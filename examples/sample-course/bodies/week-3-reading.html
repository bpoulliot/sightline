Overview

Analysis is the part of the course where the vocabulary from module one and the procedures from module two are put to work on a single extended example. Read this page in full before starting the debate. The example runs from the collection of the original observations through to the statement of a claim, and every step is described here rather than in a separate document so that the sequence stays in one place. Begin with the observations themselves. Observations are recorded as they are made, without interpretation, and the record is kept even where an observation later turns out to be irrelevant, because a record that has been pruned cannot be audited. Once the observations are recorded they are grouped, and the grouping is the first place a judgement enters. Two observations belong together when they answer the same question, not when they look alike, and the difference between those two tests is the difference between an analysis that holds and one that merely reads well. Grouping is followed by the identification of a pattern. A pattern is a statement that holds across a group and would fail if any member of the group were removed; that failure condition is what makes it checkable. Patterns that survive the removal of any single member are usually restatements of the grouping and should be discarded. From a pattern a claim is drawn, and a claim is the only part of the sequence that goes beyond what was observed. A claim carries a scope, and the scope is where most weak analyses fail: the claim is true of the observations and is stated as though it were true of everything. State the scope explicitly, in the same sentence as the claim, so that a reader cannot mistake one for the other. Finally the claim is tested against observations that were not part of the group it was drawn from. If it holds, the analysis stands. If it does not, the failure is recorded alongside the claim rather than replacing it, because a claim with a recorded exception is more useful than a claim that has been quietly narrowed until nothing contradicts it. Work through the extended example in the attached slides, and bring one disagreement to the debate.
